Downstream of marine cloud brightening

This map shows the downstream effects of marine cloud brightening (MCB) in a climate model. Hirasawa et al. (2023) simulated MCB over three subtropical ocean regions in CESM2 under the SSP2-4.5 scenario. They evaluated the response of precipitation, temperature and 13 climate tipping elements, from Amazon rainforest drought to Arctic sea-ice loss. Values are model outputs, 2034–2043.

About

Marine cloud brightening (MCB) is a proposed form of solar radiation modification. Sea-salt aerosol is sprayed into low marine clouds to raise their cloud droplet number concentration. This makes the clouds more reflective and cools the surface below. MCB has been proposed to offset part of global warming, or to protect specific regions such as coral reefs. Outdoor tests so far have been small in scale.

This map shows the results of a study of the downstream effects of MCB in one climate model. Hirasawa et al. (2023) applied MCB in the Community Earth System Model version 2 (CESM2; Danabasoglu et al. 2020) over three subtropical regions of marine stratocumulus cloud, individually and in combination:

  • NE Pacific (NEP): 0–30°N, 110–150°W (west of Mexico)
  • SE Pacific (SEP): 30°S–0, 70–110°W (off Peru and northern Chile)
  • SE Atlantic (SEA): 30°S–0, 25°W–15°E (off Angola and Namibia)

Each experiment runs from 2015 to 2064 under the SSP2-4.5 emissions scenario (O'Neill et al. 2016) and has three ensemble members. An ensemble member is one run of the model with the same forcing but slightly different initial conditions. Each member therefore has its own internal variability, such as different El Niño years, and averaging across members separates the forced response from that variability. The three MCB runs start from three members of the CESM2 Large Ensemble (Rodgers et al. 2021). The comparison without MCB uses that ensemble's members: 16 for SSP2-4.5 and 50 for the historical period.

The authors evaluated the response of 13 climate tipping elements. These are components of the climate system that may cross a threshold into a different, possibly irreversible state (Armstrong McKay et al. 2022), and each is represented by one indicator.

MCB is represented in an idealised way. The model does not simulate sea-salt emissions or how the particles form cloud droplets. Instead, in each region it sets the cloud droplet number concentration of liquid clouds over the ocean to 600 cm⁻³, several times typical values over the remote ocean. The experiments therefore show how the climate system responds to a large, prescribed brightening of these clouds. They don't represent a particular spraying technology or deployment plan.

In this model the remote response depends strongly on the region. MCB in the SE Pacific increases Amazon precipitation and reduces the Amazon water deficit (MCWD +73.7 mm). MCB in the SE Atlantic has the opposite effect (−73.2 mm), while producing almost no global-mean cooling (−0.02 °C in 2034–2043).

Method

All values are differences in decadal means for 2034–2043. The MCB response is the mean of the three MCB ensemble members minus the SSP2-4.5 ensemble mean without MCB, from the CESM2 Large Ensemble. "None (warming alone)" shows the SSP2-4.5 ensemble mean for 2034–2043 minus the historical ensemble mean for 1995–2014. The authors chose 2034–2043 as the decade in which MCB in all three regions offsets global-mean warming relative to 1995–2014. The data shown here reproduce this: −1.08 °C from MCB against +1.08 °C of warming.

Precipitation is total precipitation (PRECT: convective and large-scale, rain and snow), in mm/day. Temperature is 2 m air temperature (TREFHT). Data are shown on the model's native grid of about 0.94° × 1.25°. Coastlines are the model's land mask, where land fraction is above 0.5.

Colour

Colour classes are 0.25 mm/day wide for precipitation and 0.5 °C wide for temperature. The central white class spans ±0.125 mm/day or ±0.25 °C. Cells where the change isn't statistically significant at the 5% level are shown in lighter colours. The test is a two-sided two-sample t-test with pooled standard deviation, following the authors' analysis code. A full-strength white cell is therefore a small but statistically significant change, while a lighter colour marks a change that can't be distinguished from the model's internal variability.

Tipping elements

Each tipping element is represented by one indicator averaged over a region, with a direction of change that the authors associate with increased tipping risk: for example, a weaker AMOC or a lower Amazon water-deficit value. Soft grey box outlines show the sign of the effect on tipping risk for the selected brightened region. The outline is thickest and darkest for increased risk, medium for no significant change (p ≥ 0.05), and thinnest and lightest for reduced risk. Significance uses the same t-test on decadal means, comparing the 3 MCB members with 16 SSP2-4.5 members. The hovered or selected tipping element is outlined in orange. Brightened regions are shown as white bands with a soft grey-blue edge.

Limitations

  • Results are from a single model, CESM2. Regional precipitation responses to MCB vary between models. Jones et al. (2009), using a Met Office Hadley Centre model, also found that MCB over the South Atlantic reduced Amazon precipitation. That is consistent with the SE Atlantic result here, but agreement on other regional responses has not been tested on this map.
  • MCB is prescribed as a fixed cloud droplet number concentration (600 cm⁻³) rather than simulated from sea-salt emissions. The uncertainties of aerosol–cloud interactions are therefore not represented. These include cloud adjustments that can offset or reverse the brightening (Feingold et al. 2024).
  • Three ensemble members per experiment limit how well the forced response can be separated from internal variability. Some non-significant results may hide real responses. With 13 indicators and 4 experiments tested at the 5% level, two or three false positives would be expected by chance.
  • Forcing is constant in fixed regions for 50 years. It does not represent seasonal, adaptive or interrupted deployment, including termination.
  • "Increased risk" refers to the direction of change of each indicator as defined by the authors. It does not mean that a threshold is crossed. For example, the authors count increased Sahel precipitation as increased risk for the Sahel / West African monsoon ("greening") tipping element, a change that may be beneficial locally.
  • The indicators describe physical climate variables. They don't quantify impacts on people or ecosystems.

  • Processing. Following the authors' notebook: decadal means for 2034–2043 (and 1995–2014), mean of the MCB members minus mean without MCB, and a pooled two-sample t-test.
